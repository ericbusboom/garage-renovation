## 1. What this study answers

The recorded design direction ([DEC-004](../../00-project-controls/decision-log.md)) exposes the painted steel frame and places metal wall panels inside it. Four questions follow, and this study addresses each:

1. **Should the panels carry lateral load?** The frame currently does not rely on them. Section 5.
2. **Do the panels run continuous across the inside face, or sit inside each bay?** Section 4 — and this turns out to be the decision that constrains material choice more than anything else.
3. **What panel products actually exist, and what do they cost?** Sections 2, 3, 6.
4. **Which bays could be glass, and what glazing systems suit a 4-inch steel frame?** Section 7.

A fifth issue emerged during research and is treated as a finding rather than a question: **the site is in a marine corrosion zone, and this disqualifies the default substrate.** Section 8. Revision 1 carries that finding through to the roof at owner request, in section 8A.

### On the "waviness" the owner described

Three distinct looks are available and they are not interchangeable:

- **⅞" corrugated** — sinusoidal, continuous, no flats. This is the recognisable wavy profile. Cheapest of everything at $1–3/sq ft material, universally stocked, impact-resistant, and reads as both agricultural and contemporary depending on finish. It is also the only profile in this study that a competent owner-builder can install without specialist trade.
- **Box rib / R-panel** — flat fields separated by trapezoidal ribs. Crisper and more industrial; the 7.2 box rib is the strongest single-skin wall profile in the surveyed range and is the usual choice when the panel is also meant to span. R-panel runs 15–20% above corrugated, and roughly half of standing seam.
- **Deep sculpted profiles on aluminum** — Pure + FreeForm markets seven named corrugation profiles (Adder, Block, Canyon, Peak, Ravine, Sharkfin, Terrain) on 0.8 mm and 1 mm aluminum, with 17+ stocked finishes including blackened steel, bronze, weathered-rust, stone, and woodgrain, plus AAMA 2605 coating, ASTM E84 Class A, and a 20-year warranty. Micro-perforation with acoustic backing is available. This is the family that matches "patterned or textured, in-and-out waviness" most directly, and it is also the family that requires a quotation rather than a catalogue price.

Foam backing for insulation, which the owner raised as a possibility, is exactly what family 3 is. It is not an add-on to families 1, 2, or 5 — for those the insulation goes in the wall cavity behind, which is what the current estimate already provides ($7,486–$11,055 for studs, R-19 batt, vapour barrier, and 5/8" Type X).

### IMP specifics, since that is the current basis

Metl-Span CF Mesa, taken as representative: thicknesses 2, 2.5, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8 inch; widths 30, 36, 42, 44 inch; R-17.5 at 2 inch rising to R-70 at 8 inch; G-90 galvanised or AZ-50 Galvalume skins in 26, 24, or 22 gauge; offset double tongue-and-groove with no exposed fasteners; explicitly rated for exterior walls, **interior walls, and ceilings**; horizontal or vertical installation. Kingspan's range spans 1–8 inch at R-7.2/inch for PIR and R-8.0/inch for QuadCore.

At 2 inch and R-17.5, the IMP alone is close to the R-19 batt wall already in the estimate. If IMP is selected, the batt-and-drywall assembly behind it is partly redundant insulation — though see Section 9, because it may still be required for other reasons.

**Not one bay is a whole multiple of any standard width.** The near misses are 131.75 ÷ 44 = 2.99 and 59.00 ÷ 30 = 1.97.

This is decisive. A tongue-and-groove IMP or a concealed-clip single-skin panel derives its weather seal and its concealed fastening *from its factory side joint*. Cut one to an odd width to fit a bay and you cut that joint off, and every bay then needs a bespoke closure piece plus a field-formed edge. The economics and the weatherproofing both degrade.

### 4.3 The two arrangements

**Arrangement A — continuous across the inside face.** Panels run past the columns and beams on the inside, on horizontal sub-girts set off the frame. The panel module is set by the panel, not by the bay; sidelaps stay factory-formed; the run is uninterrupted. From the outside the full 4-inch depth of every member reads clear, which serves the decorative intent directly. The penalty: the panel plane is offset inboard by the sub-girt depth plus panel thickness, so the frame is fully expressed but the wall loses that depth of interior room, and the beam-to-panel junction needs a designed closure and flashing at every crossing.

**Arrangement B — set into each bay.** Each bay is its own framed opening, panel captured on all four edges by trim against the members. Reads as a coffered wall with the steel as a picture frame. This suits materials that are *cut to size and perimeter-captured rather than interlocked*: flat sheet or plate, ACM cassette, glass, polycarbonate, perforated sheet. It suits T&G IMP and concealed-clip single-skin panels poorly, for the reason above. It also multiplies the trim and sealant count by the number of bays, and every bay is a separate shop measurement.

**Arrangement C — hybrid.** Run a continuous inside-face panel, and align its reveals or its rib spacing to the column centres so the field reads as bays without being built as bays. The existing visualisation studies already assume something close to this: light grey 2-inch insulated infill panels with expressed vertical joints at 24 in. Note that 24 in does not divide 135.75, 115.25, or 84 either, so if joint-to-column alignment is wanted it needs to be drawn, not assumed.

Whichever is chosen, **both arrangements need secondary framing.** A girt is the horizontal member that gives the panel lateral support against wind; sub-girts set the spacing, alignment, and load transfer between panel and primary frame. None of this secondary steel is in STR-006, which excludes secondary joists, rafters, diaphragms, bracing design, fasteners, and foundations, and it is not separately priced in the current estimate.

### 4.4 Recommendation for review

Arrangement A or C for the opaque field, Arrangement B for the glazed and feature bays. This lets the cheap, well-sealed, factory-jointed product run continuously where it does the weatherproofing work, and reserves bay-by-bay infill for the places where the picture-frame effect is the point and where the material is cut-to-size anyway.

## 5. Can the panels provide rigidity?

Short answer: **some panel types can, the currently assumed type cannot, and recruiting them is probably not worth what it costs the design.**

### 5.1 IMP is disqualified

Insulated metal panels are not designed for use as bearing walls or shear diaphragm walls; they are cladding systems, not structural support systems. Unlike structural insulated panels, IMPs do not provide shear resistance to the wall assembly, though they do resist transverse loads such as wind. They are normally installed on steel frames that are already braced and provide all necessary lateral support. Where IMPs do have structural strength, capacity is typically governed by the fastener system under negative wind load.

So if the 2-inch IMP in the current cost basis is retained, the rigidity question is closed: the frame carries everything lateral, as STR-006 already assumes.

### 5.2 Profiled single-skin sheet can be designed as a diaphragm

There is a real, codified path. ANSI/AISI S310 is the North American Standard for the Design of Profiled Steel Diaphragm Panels; it covers diaphragms **and wall diaphragms** built from fluted panels, deck, and cellular deck, gives shear strength and shear stiffness provisions, and permits supports of steel, wood, or concrete with or without insulation between panel and support. SDI's Diaphragm Design Manual, 4th edition (DDM04), is the companion design manual covering strength, stiffness, fasteners, connections, and warping.

Published test data for sheet-steel-sheathed shear walls indicates allowable shear from roughly **500 plf to 2,300 plf ASD**, varying with sheet thickness and screw size, and that capacity rises sharply as edge fastener spacing tightens — one test series held field screws at 12 in while reducing edge spacing from 6 in to 2 in, with large strength gains. Corrugated sheathing with slit patterns can be tuned for ductility alongside high strength and initial stiffness. The governing failure mode is screws popping out as the sheet warps, and screws gouging elongated holes in the framing under cyclic racking. AISI S310 and DDM04 cap the fastener contribution at 25% of total diaphragm strength.

Note that most of this literature is cold-formed steel framing (AISI S400 governs seismic design of CFS shear walls and added steel-sheet sheathing provisions). This frame is HSS. The panel-to-frame connection would therefore be a bespoke engineered detail, not a tabulated assembly, and the load path from panel through sub-girt to HSS to base plate to pier would all need design.

### 5.3 Why it is probably not worth it here

Four arguments against, for the owner's consideration:

1. **It contradicts the glass.** Section 7 contemplates substantial glazing. A shear panel cannot have a hole in it. Every bay promoted to structural is a bay that can never become a window, and the choice would have to be frozen before the engineer's lateral design, ahead of the architectural design freeze.
2. **It contradicts the cheap options.** The panels that can be designed as diaphragms are the exposed-fastener profiled sheets — and they need close edge fastener spacing in a visible field. A 2-inch edge screw pitch on an interior feature wall is a strong aesthetic commitment.
3. **The frame is already there.** Five truss families plus 13 columns are modelled. Bracing is unresolved, but adding lateral capacity by tuning member sizes and a defined bracing scheme is ordinary engineering; adding it by cladding diaphragm introduces an unusual assembly for a plan-check in a high-seismic jurisdiction.
4. **It transfers risk to the finish.** A structural cladding panel cannot be replaced, reconfigured, or drilled through later without re-analysis. On a building whose interior use is storage and equipment handling with a 1,000 lb trolley, that is a real operational constraint.

The conservative position — and the one consistent with STR-006's stated limits — is to keep the panels non-structural, design them for component-and-cladding wind pressure only, and let the frame and an explicit bracing scheme do the lateral work. If the engineer later finds a diaphragm contribution useful, the codified path exists and is documented above.

## 8. Marine corrosion — a finding that constrains substrate choice

The site is 1370 Wilbur Avenue, San Diego 92109 (Pacific Beach), within short distance of both the Pacific surf line and Mission Bay, both saltwater.

Published Galvalume warranties **exclude structures within 1,500 ft of salt water, marine atmospheres, or salt spray**, with at least one manufacturer drawing the line at two miles. Within that zone a standard Galvalume or steel panel may carry no meaningful finish or substrate warranty at all; in some cases coverage drops from 25 years to 5 years within a mile of saltwater. The recommended substrate inside the exclusion zone is **painted aluminum with a coastal-rated paint warranty**, because aluminum forms a passive oxide layer that resists salt-air corrosion.

This matters immediately, because:

- The representative IMP in Section 2 offers G-90 galvanised or **AZ-50 Galvalume** skins. Both are steel. Aluminum-skin IMP is a narrower market with a price premium.
- The designer-profile family (Section 2, family 5) is 100% aluminum by construction, which makes it the substrate-correct choice as well as the aesthetic one.
- ⅞" corrugated and the other single-skin profiles are available in aluminum as well as steel, so the cheap route survives the constraint — but the aluminum version, not the stocked Galvalume version.

**Action:** the exact distance to mean high tide for both the ocean and Mission Bay must be established from the survey, and each candidate manufacturer's written coastal exclusion checked against it, before any substrate is specified. This extends [OI-011](../../00-project-controls/open-items.md).

Because the panels are inside a weathertight envelope rather than fully exposed, a case may exist for a less corrosion-resistant substrate. That case has to be argued against the actual assembly — a vented cavity behind a panel in a coastal marine atmosphere is not a benign interior — and it is not argued here.

## 8A. The same finding applied to the roof

Added at owner request 2026-09-17. The roof is the more exposed surface, and the finding lands harder there than on the walls.

### 8A.1 What is currently assumed

[COST-ESTIMATE.md](../../../COST-ESTIMATE.md) item G carries 713 sq ft of 22-ga corrugated metal roof deck, self-adhered underlayment, and standing seam metal roof panels at $6.00-9.00/sq ft, plus 131 sq ft of standing seam hip cap at $8-12/sq ft and 60 lin ft of gutter. No substrate is named. The unstated default for standing seam at that price point is painted Galvalume, which is the product the 1,500-ft exclusion applies to.

### 8A.2 Why the roof is the harder case

Three reasons the wall conclusion does not simply carry over.

**The roof is not sheltered.** Trade guidance is explicit that Galvalume "will not stand up to" coastal conditions, that saltwater degrades the coating and causes premature damage, and that aluminum is the recommended substrate. Whatever argument exists for a steel panel inside a weathertight wall cavity does not exist for the outermost surface of the building.

**Most of the roof will never be rain-washed.** This is the finding that matters most. Exposed roof areas can corrode *more slowly* than sheltered ones, because rain periodically rinses salt away; sheltered and covered areas get no such rinse, accumulate salt, and corrode faster. Panel overlaps, flashings, undersides, gutters, and specifically the area *beneath solar equipment* retain deposits. Light rain can make it worse, dissolving salt and then leaving fresh deposits as it evaporates.

On this building that zone is not a detail. Using SOL-001's array footprint of 273.5 x 143.2 in (272 sq ft) against its 325 sq ft south face, **the modules shelter roughly 84% of the south slope** and about 38% of the whole 713 sq ft roof. The south slope is also the slope that faces the prevailing marine air. A substrate chosen on the assumption that rain keeps it clean is being chosen for conditions that will not exist across most of this roof.

Note that the roof area bases do not currently reconcile: item G's 713 sq ft derives from the older 30-degree geometry (282 x 214 in south, slope run 17.83 ft, plus 282 x 150 in north, run 12.50 ft), while ASM-SOL-01 records a 325 sq ft south face at 35 degrees with a 13.86 ft run. The percentages above should be recomputed once the roof geometry is frozen.

**Access for washing is poor and gets worse.** The remedy trade sources give for salt accumulation is periodic rinsing. Under a fixed PV array on a 30-35 degree slope, two storeys up, that is not a realistic owner maintenance task, and the solar report already notes that required fire access, roof edges, clamps and waterproofing may invalidate the current module layout.

### 8A.3 What changes

| Item | Current basis | Coastal position |
|---|---|---|
| Standing seam substrate | unstated; Galvalume implied by price | Painted aluminum, 0.032-0.040 in, with a coastal-rated paint warranty |
| Paint warranty | not stated | Aluminum PVDF is typically 40 yr standard, **25 yr coastal**; Galvalume substrate perforation warranty is excluded within 1,500 ft |
| Weathertight warranty | not stated | Available on aluminum including coastal; Galvalume weathertight warranties are non-coastal |
| Clips and fasteners | in the $6-9/sf line | 316 stainless, not 304 or 18-8; 316 resists the chloride attack and tea-staining that fail 304 in coastal service, at a 20-30% fastener premium |
| Gutters, 60 lin ft | unstated at $8-12/lin ft | Aluminum or stainless, not galvanized |
| PV attachment | not detailed | Non-penetrating seam clamps with round-point setscrews; Class III 20+ micron anodising for coastal |
| Module-to-roof isolation | not addressed | Non-conductive isolation pads (EPDM, neoprene) at every dissimilar-metal contact |
| Corrugated 22-ga deck | steel, unstated coating | Protected by the underlayment, but condensation on its underside and its galvanic relationship to an aluminum panel above need the envelope consultant's review |

### 8A.4 Galvanic pairing, which the substrate switch partly solves

Galvalume performs best when not in contact with concrete, treated lumber, copper, lead, bricks, or iron. The PV array introduces aluminum module frames and aluminum rails onto whatever the roof is made of, in the presence of a salt electrolyte, which is the classic accelerated-corrosion setup. In marine ISO C4/C5 exposure, galvanized steel racking components can have a service life of only 5-20 years against a 25-year PV system design life.

An aluminum roof panel under aluminum rails and aluminum module frames is the compatible pairing, which is a second, independent argument for the substrate switch. It does not eliminate the need for isolation pads at stainless-to-aluminum contacts, because aluminum is the less noble metal there and will sacrifice itself to protect the stainless.

### 8A.5 Cost

Material only, per published ranges: painted Galvalume $1.75-2.35/sq ft against aluminum $2.20-3.00/sq ft. Over 713 sq ft that is a **$321-463** difference in raw material.

Installed is where the estimate moves. Two methods, both shown because they disagree:

| Method | Main roof, 713 sq ft | Hip cap, 131 sq ft |
|---|---|---|
| Current estimate, item G | $4,278-6,417 | $1,048-1,572 |
| Aluminum at a 20-30% premium | $5,134-8,342 | $1,258-2,044 |
| Aluminum at published $9-18/sq ft installed | $6,417-12,834 | not separately published |

On the premium method the roof subtotal moves from $9,601-14,944 to roughly **$10,666-17,341**, a delta of about **$1,065-2,397**, before the 316 stainless fastener premium and the isolation pads. The absolute $9-18/sq ft range would push it further; the spread between the two methods is itself a reason to get a real quotation rather than settle this from published ranges.

Set against a PV and battery package at $30,475-39,095 gross and a 25-year design life, a one-to-two-thousand-dollar substrate decision on the surface the array is bolted to is not where this project should economise.

### 8A.6 Two things the switch costs

**Thermal movement.** Aluminum expands and contracts roughly twice as much as steel, and panels over 15-20 ft need expansion clips to avoid oil canning and noise. The south slope run at the 30-degree basis is 17.83 ft, squarely inside that band; the 35-degree basis gives 13.86 ft, outside it. So whether the south slope needs floating clips depends on which roof geometry is frozen, and the north slope at 12.50 ft does not. This must be settled with the roof geometry, not after.

**Denting.** Aluminum is more malleable and more susceptible to surface denting during handling and installation. On a roof that will be walked for PV installation, module cleaning and future replacement, that argues for the heavier 0.040 in sheet and for defined walk paths, not the thinnest warrantable gauge.

### 8A.7 Position for review

Specify painted aluminum standing seam with a coastal-rated paint warranty, 316 stainless clips and fasteners, aluminum or stainless gutters, non-penetrating seam clamps for the array, and isolation pads at every dissimilar-metal contact. Obtain the written coastal warranty terms before ordering, because the 1,500-ft line is a manufacturer-by-manufacturer term and one surveyed manufacturer draws it at two miles.

The roof warrants this more strongly than the walls do, and unlike the walls it cannot be revisited later: the panels will be under a fixed PV array, and replacing them means removing and reinstalling the array.

## 9. Fire and code items the panel choice triggers

Not determinations. Items for the architect and plan-check.

1. **Foam plastic core and thermal barrier.** IBC/CBC Chapter 26 requires foam plastic to be separated from the building interior by an approved thermal barrier: ½-inch gypsum wallboard, heavy timber, or a material meeting the acceptance criteria of both the Temperature Transmission and Integrity fire tests of NFPA 275. An exception exists by special approval after large-scale testing — NFPA 286, FM 4880, UL 1040, or UL 1715 — and this exception is commonly relied on for composite foam insulated metal panels. **If IMP is selected, the specific panel's large-scale test approval must be obtained, or the thermal barrier retained.** Note also that where a thermal barrier was present in an assembly's fire test, it cannot be omitted in the real installation and must run the full wall height.
2. **NFPA 285.** This test governs flammability of exterior non-load-bearing wall assemblies containing foam plastic insulation. Applicability depends on construction type and height and should be confirmed rather than assumed for an accessory structure — but if it applies, the tested assembly, not just the panel, is what complies.
3. **The Type X drywall already in the estimate.** 778 sq ft of 5/8" Type X is carried for fire code. It plausibly doubles as the Chapter 26 thermal barrier. If the drywall is deleted as redundant insulation once an IMP is chosen, the thermal-barrier and fire-separation functions must be re-established by another means. **Do not delete that line item on thermal grounds alone.**
4. **Coastal Overlay Zone.** [Document requirements](../../00-project-controls/document-requirements.md) already list a Coastal Overlay Zone and coastal-development-permit determination, and [OI-002](../../00-project-controls/open-items.md) is open. An overlay may carry appearance or materials expectations that bear on a visible exterior frame and panel colour.
